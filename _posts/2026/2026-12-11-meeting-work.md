# You Want a Meeting or You Want the Work Done?

Back in 2015, a group of researchers wrote a paper with one of the best titles I have ever read, [“You Want My Password or a Dead Patient?”](https://web.archive.org/web/20151203034758/https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/~sws/pubs/ksbk15-draft.pdf)[^paper] It is about doctors and nurses, and the security rules built by people who had never worked a hospital shift.

The workarounds are glorious. Passwords on sticky notes stuck to the very devices they protect. A Styrofoam cup placed over the proximity sensor so the computer never notices the clinician has walked away. The most junior person on the floor assigned to tap the space bar so nobody gets logged out. One doctor whose thumbprint was the only one the reader would accept, so he signed the death certificates for the entire hospital.

The authors are careful to say these are not villains. “These are not terrorists or black hat hackers, but rather clinicians trying to use the computer system for conventional healthcare activities.” The rules were in the way of the work, and the work won, because “the medical mission trumps the security mission.”

That story was an instant reminder while reading a recent Hacker News thread, [“Why do we have so many meetings?”](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50167732), on an NBER paper simply titled [Meetings](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35706).[^nber] Because most of us do not work in hospitals, and nobody dies when the meeting runs long. But the shape is the same; a ritual that was supposed to serve the work has quietly started to replace it.

> “Do you Want a Meeting, or do you Want the Work Done?”

## The meeting is the work, apparently

There is a certain kind of colleague, and you have already pictured one, for whom the meeting is the job. Send them a clear, two-paragraph note that explains the problem, the proposed fix, and what you need from them, and the reply arrives within minutes: “Let’s discuss this.” Not a question. Not an objection. Not a yes. A calendar invite, for Friday, thirty minutes, with six people on it, two of whom have never heard of the project.

One commenter on that thread put it plain and simple: some people “refuse to do anything unless there is a meeting.” Another described turning a yes-or-no question into a “quick call,” which is how five minute becomes thirty minutes, and then, if the organizer is feeling generous at the end, a speech about “giving you some time back,” as if it had been theirs to give.

And then there are the attendees who are not there to decide anything. They are there to be seen to be there. A full calendar is the corporate equivalent of a peacock’s tail: expensive, impractical, and entirely for display. If you want to see real anger in an office, it is about a meeting someone was not invited to.

The thread also reminded me of Wadge’s Law, which I had not heard named before: before every formal meeting there is a smaller, more exclusive, less formal meeting where the important decisions are made. Sometimes there is a pre-pre-meeting. It is meetings all the way down, and the one on your calendar is the one where everybody performs the decision that was already made.

## The uncomfortable part: it works

Here is where it stops being funny. The NBER paper, surveying over nine thousand workers, found that meetings take about 12% of working hours and around 14% of a firm’s wage bill. It also found that people who spend more time in meetings see faster wage growth. You can read that as meetings being where knowledge moves from senior people to junior ones, which is what the authors suggest and which is sometimes true. You can also read it the way a few of the commenters did: meetings are the most powerful channel for being noticed, and being noticed is, unfortunately, half of a career.

That is the Styrofoam cup again. When the system rewards being visible over being useful, people will route around the work to be visible.

> That meeting could have been an email

That meeting could have been an email. That email could have been a chat message. That chat message could have been a line in the document everyone already has open. And quite often, that document did not need anyone’s permission at all, it needed someone to just make the change and say so.

Each step down that ladder is cheaper for everyone else. A meeting takes an hour from six people at the same time. An email takes two minutes from each of them, whenever they get to it. A chat takes ten seconds. The work, the actual thing, takes however long it takes, and it is the only one of these that ships.

To be fair to the meeting, there are good ones. A decision that is truly stuck, a hard conversation that should never be typed, a new team member who needs to hear the history from the people who lived it, or a crisis where everyone needs the same picture at the same moment. Those deserve a room. They are also rare, and the good ones have an agenda, a written document going in, and a decision coming out. If none of those three exist, what you have is not a meeting; it is a gathering.

## Manage yourself first

The cure is not a policy. Every “No Meeting Wednesday” I have seen survives about six weeks before it becomes “No Meeting Wednesday (except for the important ones),” which is all of them.

The cure is people who can manage themselves. Who read the note. Who reply with an answer instead of an invitation. Who write down what they are doing, so nobody needs a status meeting to find out. Who make the small decisions they are allowed to make, and own them when they are wrong. A lot of meetings exist only because someone, somewhere, is not sure they are allowed to decide, so they gather everyone who might have an opinion and wait for the room to decide for them.

And then, manage others the same way. Being a team player is not attending everything. It is being the person others can count on without having to schedule you. It is unblocking a colleague with a two-line reply before lunch, so they do not need to book your Thursday. A good team rarely meets because it rarely needs to; everyone already knows what everyone else is doing, because they wrote it down.

The doctors in that paper did not stick passwords on monitors because they were careless. They did it because they had patients to treat, and the system had forgotten that. Most of our work is not life or death. But it is still work, and somebody still has to do it.

So, do you want the meeting, or do you want the work done?
