# How to Read a Book

> Or, what forty years of reading did not teach me.

After decades of reading books, I have decided to finally learn [How to Read a Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Read_a_Book) by [Mortimer J. Adler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortimer_J._Adler). There is a particular kind of embarrassment in that sentence, and I have made my peace with it.

Adler published it in 1940. He revised it in 1972 with [Charles Van Doren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Van_Doren), and that is the edition most of us hold. It is not a book about loving books. It is closer to a manual, written by a man who believed reading is a skill, that most adults quietly stopped learning it somewhere around the sixth grade, and that this was worth being blunt about.

His central move is to split reading into four levels, each one folded into the next.

**Elementary** is what school gave you. What does the sentence say? Most of us stopped here and have been coasting ever since.

**Inspectional** is reading on a budget. Systematic skimming — the table of contents, the index, the preface, a paragraph here and there — to answer one question: what kind of book is this, and does it deserve more of my life? Adler is emphatic that this is a real skill and not cheating. Most books only ever need this.

**Analytical** is the heart of the book and most of its pages. You classify the book, work out its structure, come to terms with the author — literally, agree on what his words mean before you argue with them — and only then judge it. The rule I keep returning to is his one about disagreement: you must be able to say “I understand” before you are allowed to say “I agree,” “I disagree,” or “I suspend judgment.” Most arguments on the internet die at that first hurdle.

**Syntopical** is reading many books on one subject and building an understanding that sits in none of them individually. It is the hardest, and it is the one that actually changes how you think.

Underneath all four sit his four questions. What is this book about as a whole? What is being said in detail, and how? Is it true, in whole or in part? And, my favorite, *what of it?* — the question that quietly separates a book you have read from a book that has done something to you.

## Is it any good?

Honestly, it is a slog. There is a real irony in a book about reading being this hard to finish, and I don’t think Adler would have minded me saying so. It is repetitive, it is fond of its own taxonomy, and the 137-title reading list at the back is Homer-to-Solzhenitsyn Western canon in a way that has aged into a period piece.

But I kept the ideas. Inspectional reading gave me permission to stop apologizing for skimming. “Come to terms with the author” has made me a better arguer and a much slower one. And *what of it?* has quietly retired a shelf of books I was keeping out of guilt.

The rest of what follows is mine, accumulated the slow way, before I read him and after.

## Why Read?

1. Reading well will deepen your appreciation of literature; you’ll gain new experiences and enrich your knowledge of the world and other people.
2. Reading well requires noticing details, reflecting on them and questioning them.
3. One key to reading well and pleasurably is to slow down, take your time and read deliberatively.
4. Re-reading literary texts will allow you to pick up on what you missed the first time around and deepen your reading pleasure.
5. Reading aloud, whether a sentence or an entire page, will help you notice more about a writer’s craft and art, especially how the tone and texture complement the meaning.

## How to read books the right way

1. Pick up multiple books (physical, digital, or audio) from the same topic or category to go deeper. This is Adler’s syntopical reading, and you can start it without his permission.
2. It is OK not to finish a book you don’t like.
3. Some books are more flexible and might not be read linearly. It does depend on the topic of book you’re reading. It’s okay to pick up chapters if the book is meant to be a collection of answers to unrelated topics.
4. Write notes, highlight them, or stick tiny stickies on pages (for physical books).
5. You can speed-read[^speed-read] parts and sections that might not be useful to remember.
6. While reading you might come across topics you don’t know much about. While you’re still finishing the book, read a brief summary to get through the current content. Write them down separately to research more later.
7. Try to write a summary after you’ve read the whole book.
8. Find your format/style or mix them up — digital, physical, audio.

### Tips

Here are a few personal tips that worked for me while trying to read and learn the contents of books and get the best out of them. This will help you overcome the habit of `Tsundoku`[^tsundoku].

- Read reviews, from multiple sources, of a book and try to know a general idea of the content and idea of that book.
- Or listen to audio books at about 1.2x speed. You can use whatever you are comfortable with — a headphone, or in your car.
- Once you have either read the reviews or heard the audio book, decide if you want to read the book or not. When you do read it, either physical or digital, but now you know which part to browse and which part to concentrate on.

That last one is inspectional reading wearing a disguise, which I did not know until I read Adler. I had spent years arriving at a worse version of a method somebody wrote down before my parents were born. That is either humbling or reassuring, and most days I take it as both.
